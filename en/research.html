Why study bacteria in food?

Some bacteria found in meat, milk and other foods can cause serious illness. Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC) and Salmonella are among the most important. Controlling them requires knowing where they are, what makes them dangerous and how they survive cleaning and preservation methods.

We read the complete genome of these bacteria (genome sequencing) and combine it with laboratory testing. This helps us understand the risk posed by each strain and find better ways to keep food safe, from farm to fork.

Line 1

STEC virulence and genomics

How Shiga toxin-producing E. coli causes disease, why some diagnostic tests fail, and what genomes reveal about cattle strains linked to outbreaks. Includes work on "super-shedder" animals and on the environmental factors that trigger toxin production.

Key papers: PLOS ONE 2021 · Frontiers in Microbiology 2023 · Trends in Food Science & Technology 2022

Line 2

Pathogen control and sanitizer resistance

We test disinfectants, organic acids, UV-C light and heat against STEC and multidrug-resistant Salmonella. We also study the genes that let bacteria tolerate sanitizers, and whether this is linked to their ability to cause disease.

Key papers: Antibiotics 2025 · Food Microbiology 2023 · Pathogens 2020

Line 3

Pathogens in the Brazilian food chain

Occurrence, antimicrobial resistance and genomics of STEC and Salmonella in meat and animal production, with a focus on Mato Grosso, plus reviews of the Brazilian scenario.

Key papers: Microorganisms 2019 · Tropical Animal Health and Production 2019 · Genes 2025

Line 4 New

Applied physical chemistry, electrochemistry and bioinformatics

When microorganisms spoil food, they release characteristic vapors and gases. In this new line at UFMT, we measure these compounds with low-cost, Arduino-based electrochemical sensors. The data feed predictive and machine learning models that estimate how far spoilage has progressed.
